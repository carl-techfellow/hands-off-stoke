What This Game Is

Prompt Pictionary is a ten-minute partner game. One person is dealt five secret task cards and picks one — something like "a horror story about a vending machine, in exactly 3 sentences." They write a prompt asking an AI to produce it, without using that card's banned words. Then they show their partner only what the AI produced — never the card, never the prompt. The partner has 45 seconds to guess what the original task was. Then you swap.

It's Pictionary, except the drawing is whatever the AI made, and the quality of that "drawing" depends entirely on how precisely the first person wrote.

What It Strengthens

Four things, all of which show up in the actual work within a week:

  • Writing an instruction someone else can act on. The AI can't ask a clarifying question. Neither can a teammate reading your documentation at 11pm, or a client reading your handoff after you've left.
  • Specificity over familiarity. The most common failure is an instruction that feels complete because the writer is silently supplying context from their own head. You find out instantly here, because the AI supplies nothing.
  • Format and constraint as part of the ask. Cards state a length on purpose — "in exactly 3 sentences," "in 40 words or fewer." Carrying that constraint into the prompt is the difference between a usable result and a wall of text.
  • Noticing where meaning gets lost. Intent → prompt → AI output → someone else's guess. Something can drop at every step, and the guess at the end makes it visible instead of theoretical.

The scoring is deliberately light — a running "guessed / played" count per session, not a leaderboard. It's there so a pair can notice themselves getting better across rounds, which is the actual signal worth chasing.

For Whoever's Running This

Everything above the fold is the app two people actually use. This part is for you — the plain version of the same rules, plus what to have ready.

The Rules, Plainly

  • Pair up, across teams, one device each.
  • A is dealt five random cards and picks one — a finished text thing with a stated length (a 3-line haiku, a 3-sentence horror story, a 25-word warning label). B doesn't see it, and doesn't see A's prompt while guessing — only what the AI produced. Both get revealed at the end, side by side with the task.
  • A writes a prompt and runs it privately, trying to get the AI to make that thing. Every card bans its own giveaway words — the haiku card bans "haiku," "keys," "car," "lost" — and on top of that the prompt can't copy the card's wording, mention the game, use real data, or get a second try. The app shows the banned words and the rules on A's screen, so you don't have to police them yourself.
  • A shows only the output. B's job is to guess the task A was given — not to critique the writing — and they get 45 seconds. The app carries a red "don't share your screen yet" banner through every secret step and flips it green the moment it's safe to show, which matters most on virtual days.
  • At the reveal, all three appear together: the task, A's actual prompt, and what the AI made. That's the debrief in one screen — you can see exactly where the meaning survived or got lost, without anyone having to recall what they typed.
  • Reveal the real card. Compare. Swap and go again.

Materials Checklist

  • Everyone's own device, AI tool open and logged in beforehand — not during.
  • A way to keep A's screen private from B during steps 1–3 of the app.
  • Pairs assigned across teams ahead of time, not self-selected.
  • If virtual: breakout rooms with pairs pre-built, posted in chat before opening — A's privacy is automatic, just their own window.

Things That Go Wrong

What happensWhat you do
A's prompt basically restates the card word for word Against the rules now, not just boring — the app lists this under "your prompt can't." Have them try again: say it in their own words instead.
A names the exact format out loud ("write a haiku...") Also against the rules if it's a giveaway — redirect to describing the shape instead ("three lines, short-long-short").
B peeks at A's screen during the secret steps "Eyes on your own device until it's revealed."
A reacts visibly while B is guessing "Poker face — not one word or face until time's up."
The AI output is nothing like the card Great round, not a failed one — read the real prompt aloud and diagnose where it drifted.
"Notice the chain that just happened: A had something exact in mind. It passed through A's prompt, then through the AI's interpretation of that prompt, before B ever saw anything. Something got lost — or survived — at each step, and B only had the last link in that chain to guess from. That's the next forty minutes, except the auditor is guessing at your intent from your repo, not a poem about your car keys."

Say it once, out loud, don't repeat it, then move straight into the work.
